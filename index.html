No black box

Exactly how we teach it

Most tutoring will not tell you what happens in the room, because mostly what happens is worksheets. Here is the whole method, including why each piece is built the way it is and what the usual approach gets wrong. Open any of them.

01 A question is not finished until it is right, cold, under time, with no help
What we do
Every question your child sees has a state: open or closed. It only closes when they get it right, under exam timing, with no hint, and having said beforehand that they were confident. Anything else leaves it open, and it comes back in a later timed test with the options shuffled.
Why
Most prep counts a question as done the moment it has been marked. But a child who guessed correctly between two options has learned nothing, and the worksheet still says 100%. That is how a student can do thousands of questions and improve very little.
How
The platform tracks state per question per student. I can pull up every question still open for a child and assign exactly those as their next test in two clicks. Nothing quietly disappears because it was marked once.

Instead of: mark it, hand it back, move on, and never see that question again.

02 Test first with zero feedback. Teach second, untimed
What we do
Practice is split into two separate passes. The test pass is timed, with no hints, no explanations and no right or wrong shown. The review pass comes after, untimed, and is purely about understanding.
Why
If feedback appears the moment a child answers, they learn to lean on feedback. On exam day there is none. Splitting the passes means the timed pass is an honest measurement and the untimed pass is honest teaching, instead of a blurry mix that does neither well.
How
In review, every question they did not close walks a ladder: a hint that points at the method but never the answer, then a fresh attempt, then the written explanation, then a video solution, then a flag that lands on my desk saying "I still do not get it".

Instead of: practising with the answer key sitting open next to them.

03 Every answer is tagged with how confident they were
What we do
With every answer, your child picks one of three: confident, educated guess, or total guess. It takes one tap and it is required.
Why
A percentage score hides the two things that matter. A lucky guess looks identical to real knowledge. And confident but wrong, which is a misconception rather than a gap, is the single most dangerous state a student can be in, because they will never go back and check it.
How
Confident and wrong answers sort straight to the top of my list, and usually become the next group lesson, because a misconception is normally shared across the whole class. Lucky guesses stay open and come back until they are not lucky any more.

Instead of: a score out of 35 that tells you nothing about which marks were real.

04 Your child gets shown how accurate their own confidence is
What we do
They see a calibration score. For example: "when you said confident, you were right 71% of the time."
Why
Knowing which questions you actually know is a skill in itself, and it is worth marks directly. It decides what you skip, what you come back to, and where you spend your last five minutes. It is also what makes honest self-reporting a game they want to win rather than a rule they want to dodge.
How
Calibration is tracked across every test and shown on their home screen. A child who is 95% accurate on confident answers gets told to trust their gut and move faster. A child at 60% gets told to slow down and check.

Instead of: hoping a child works out on their own which of their answers to trust.

05 The skills are broken down and tracked individually
What we do
The exam is not "maths" and "English". It is a finite list of specific skills, and every question in the bank is tagged against that list. Each child has a mastery level on each skill: needs work, developing, or secure.
Why
"He is bad at maths" is not actionable. "He is secure on two way tables and rates, and needs work on combinatorics" is a lesson plan. Breaking the test into its parts is what turns a vague score into something you can actually fix.
How
Mastery is recalculated from rolling accuracy and from speed against a section benchmark, so a skill only counts as secure if they can do it correctly and fast. Weak skills get weighted into the next homework set automatically.

Instead of: "more practice", applied evenly, including to the things they already have.

06 Old skills come back on a spacing schedule
What we do
Once a skill is secure, it does not disappear. It resurfaces at widening intervals across the rest of the program.
Why
Forgetting is the default. Something learned in week two and never revisited is largely gone by the exam. Spaced retrieval is the single best evidenced technique in learning research for preventing that, and almost no tutoring program actually implements it because it requires tracking every student separately.
How
The scheduler builds each set from three sources: current weak skills, items due for spaced resurfacing, and a small number of stretch items above their current level. The mix is tuned in one config file, so it can be adjusted for a student who needs more of one thing.

Instead of: a linear syllabus that covers topic seven in week seven and never returns to topic one.

07 Timing is trained as its own separate skill
What we do
Every question is timed against a per section benchmark and the time is shown back to the student in review. Abstract reasoning is 35 questions in 20 minutes, which is about 35 seconds each. Quantitative and reading are about 60 seconds each.
Why
Most students lose more marks to the clock than to not knowing the content. A child who can solve every abstract reasoning question but needs 90 seconds each will finish barely half the section, and their report will wrongly say they are weak at abstract reasoning.
How
Review shows lines like "you took 2 minutes 14 seconds, you get 35 seconds in the exam". We also watch for the opposite failure, which is speeding up while getting less accurate, and flag it when a student's own trend shows it.

Instead of: untimed homework, then surprise on exam day.

08 The test craft is taught directly, not left to chance
What we do
Elimination, educated guessing, when to skip and come back, how to flag, how to transfer answers to the bubble sheet without losing your place, and what to do when you have four minutes and eight questions left.
Why
There is no penalty for a wrong answer on the ASET, which means a blank is strictly worse than a guess, and a guess after crossing off two options is worth roughly double a blind one. Kids do not know this unless someone tells them, and it is free marks.
How
The platform lets them cross options off on screen, and it records what they crossed off. So when a child guesses, I can see whether it was a coin flip between two sensible options or a shot in the dark, and teach into that specifically.

Instead of: assuming strategy is something clever kids just pick up.

09 Your child learns to run their own preparation
What we do
In the clinic, they predict their own score before they see it. They find their own weakest area in their own data rather than being told. They set their own review commitments and their own target score for the end of the program, and I audit those the following week.
Why
For a child this age, a sense of control is the variable that most affects whether they actually engage. A student who is managed works while someone is watching. A student who owns it works when nobody is. Given the exam is at the start of Year 6 and most of the work happens at home, that difference decides the outcome.
How
Homework uses bounded choice: what needs practising is fixed by the data, but the order, the pacing, the format and the quantity above a floor are theirs. They are also tracked on how accurate their own commitments were, which is a skill worth far more than this exam.

Instead of: a set homework sheet, handed over, nagged about, and resented.

Common questions

Before you reach out

What is ASET, GATE, or a scholarship exam?
ASET is the exam that decides entry into WA's selective public programs, known as GATE, including Perth Modern. Private schools run their own scholarship exams, worth up to 100% off six years of fees. Almost all of them are sat at the start of Year 6, and applications close earlier than most parents expect. If any of this is new, grab the free guide or message me and I will walk you through the whole system.
What year level is this for?
The core program is built for Year 5 students sitting Year 7 entry exams at the start of Year 6. For Term 4 we are open to enquiries from other year levels too, while we gauge demand.
Is Year 5 too early to start?
The tests are sat at the start of Year 6, and private school application lists close more than a year ahead. Year 5 is not early. It is the window.
Who actually teaches it?
Right now, me. Nathan Hubbard: I sat these exact exams, won an academic scholarship to Hale, got a 98 ATAR, and I am studying Finance and Computer Science. I built the practice platform myself. Every tutor here will have personally sat and won these exams, because nobody who has only read about them can teach the parts that matter.
Why not just go to a big tutoring centre?
At a large centre your child is one of twenty doing the same worksheet, and the writing section usually gets skipped because it is slow to mark. Here the class is four kids, there is a one-on-one clinic every week, homework is rebuilt around your child's actual errors, and you have a real number to call. See the full comparison above.
Where are classes held, and when?
In person in Perth. A group class on a weekday evening, and an individual clinic on the weekend. Message me for the current location and times.
What happens if it does not work for us?

How much homework is there?
